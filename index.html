Why molecules are the shape they are

Water is bent, carbon dioxide is straight and methane is a tetrahedron. One simple rule explains all three, and most other small molecules too. It's called VSEPR theory.

By Olly14 August 20246 min read

The one idea

Who came up with it?Nevil Sidgwick and Herbert Powell spotted the pattern in 1940. Ronald Gillespie and Ronald Nyholm turned it into the rules taught today in 1957.

VSEPR stands for valence shell electron pair repulsion, and the name is basically the whole theory. The outer electrons around a central atom come in pairs. The pairs all carry negative charge, so they repel each other, and they settle into the arrangement that keeps them as far apart as possible.

There are two kinds of pair. A bonding pair is shared between the central atom and another atom. A lone pair belongs to the central atom alone. Both count, and both push.

One detail trips people up. A double or triple bond counts once. All the electrons in a multiple bond point the same way, so they behave as one region of charge. That's why chemists often say electron domain instead of electron pair.

So the recipe is simple. Count the domains, spread them out evenly, then look at where the atoms ended up.

Counting the pairs

  1. Find the central atom and count its outer-shell (valence) electrons.
  2. Work out how many electron pairs it has, using the formula below.
  3. Pick the electron geometry from the number of pairs: 2 linear, 3 trigonal planar, 4 tetrahedral, 5 trigonal bipyramidal, 6 octahedral.
  4. Name the shape from the atoms only. Lone pairs decide where the atoms go, but they don't appear in the shape's name.

pairs = (V + M − q) ÷ 2

V
valence electrons on the central atom
M
atoms joined to it by single bonds, like H, F or Cl
q
overall charge, so a 1− ion adds one electron

Atoms joined by a double bond, like the oxygens in CO2, add nothing to M. For CO2: (4 + 0 − 0) ÷ 2 = 2 pairs.

Worked example: ammonia

N has 5 valence electrons and three H atoms: (5 + 3 − 0) ÷ 2 = 4 pairs. Three are bonding pairs, so one is a lone pair. Four pairs make a tetrahedron. With one corner taken by the lone pair, the atoms form a trigonal pyramid.

Why 109.5°?It's the angle between any two of four lines spread evenly from the centre of a sphere. In maths terms it's cos−1(−1/3).

Chemists shorten this to AX3E: A for the central atom, X for each bonded atom and E for each lone pair. Each combination gives its own shape, and there are thirteen you'll meet all the time. Here they all are.

Lone pairs take up more room

Anything with four pairs should have bond angles of 109.5°. Real molecules come close but don't quite match, and the gap tells you something.

CH4109.5°no lone pairs
NH3107°1 lone pair
H2O104.5°2 lone pairs

A bonding pair is held between two nuclei, so it's stretched out and thin. A lone pair is held by one nucleus only. It sits closer to the central atom and spreads wider, so it pushes harder. The repulsions rank like this:

lone–lone > lone–bond > bond–bond

Two other effects work the same way. Double bonds hold more electrons, so they push harder too. In methanal (H2C=O) the H–C–H angle closes to about 116° instead of 120°. Very electronegative atoms pull bonding pairs away from the centre, so those pairs need less room. NF3 has an F–N–F angle of 102°, smaller than the 107° in NH3.

Where do the lone pairs go?

With four pairs or fewer, every corner of the shape is the same, so it doesn't matter where a lone pair sits. With five, it does. A trigonal bipyramid has two kinds of position. The two axial positions, top and bottom, each have three neighbours at 90°. The three equatorial positions around the middle have only two.

Repulsion at 90° is far stronger than at 120°, so lone pairs take the roomier equatorial spots. Add them one at a time and you get a seesaw (SF4), then a T-shape (ClF3), then a straight line (XeF2).

In an octahedron every position is the same, so the first lone pair can go anywhere. That gives a square pyramid (BrF5). The second lone pair goes as far from the first as it can, directly opposite, which leaves a flat square (XeF4).

Where VSEPR breaks

VSEPR is a rule of thumb, and it has blind spots.

Heavier hydrides

H2S has an H–S–H angle of 92°, and PH3 is 93.5°. VSEPR expects something near 104° and 107°. In heavier atoms the lone pairs sit mostly in the s orbital, and the bonds use almost pure p orbitals, which are 90° apart.

Lone pairs that do nothing

[TeCl6]2− has six bonds and a lone pair (AX6E), so VSEPR says it should be distorted. It's a perfect octahedron. The lone pair stays in a spherical s orbital and doesn't point anywhere.

Transition metals

The shapes of metal complexes depend on their d electrons. VSEPR would call [Ni(CN)4]2− tetrahedral, but it's square planar because nickel(II) has eight d electrons. You need crystal field or ligand field theory for these.

For main-group molecules with lighter central atoms, though, VSEPR works well, and you can do it in your head.

Test yourself

Use the formula, then pick a shape.

XeF4

What shape is it?

Xe has 8 valence electrons and four F atoms: (8 + 4 − 0) ÷ 2 = 6 pairs. Four bond and two are lone pairs, so it's AX4E2. The lone pairs sit opposite each other, leaving a square.

ClF3

What shape is it?

Cl has 7 valence electrons and three F atoms: (7 + 3 − 0) ÷ 2 = 5 pairs. Three bond and two are lone pairs, so it's AX3E2. Both lone pairs go equatorial, which leaves a T.

I3−

What shape is it?

The middle I has 7 valence electrons, two I atoms bonded and a 1− charge: (7 + 2 + 1) ÷ 2 = 5 pairs. Two bond and three are lone pairs, so it's AX2E3. Three equatorial lone pairs leave a straight line, just like XeF2.
